Which world best times are hardest to beat?

A world best time takes a fast crew and a fast day. For each elite record I estimated how much came from each. I compared the record with a typical Worlds gold medallist from the same year, and with how fast every other event went on the same day. That gives the record's margin over today's typical gold medallist, and how often a crew like that would beat it. Further down are the unlucky and the great crews, next season's chances, how records are paced, and the countries, lakes, winds and times of day behind them.

The data covers every race at the Worlds, Olympics, World Cups, Europeans and final Olympic qualifiers since 1985: . All times, records and rankings are correct as of . New records set after that date are not included, so parts of this page will go out of date.

What stands out

Hardest: the W4-, M1x and W1x, then the M2-

A typical Worlds gold medallist in 2026 would beat the W4-, M1x or W1x record in only 1 A-final in 500 to 750. At about five A-finals a year, that works out at more than a century. Treat that as a way of saying "very unlikely to fall soon" rather than a forecast, because boats, training and the depth of each event will change long before then. The M2- is next, at about 1 in 200.

The M2- record owes the most to its crew

Murray and Bond set 6:08.50 in a heat at London 2012, on a day that was faster than average but not unusually so (75th percentile). Allowing for the conditions and for racing in a heat, they were about 4.0% faster than a typical 2012 Worlds gold medallist. That is the largest crew margin behind any current record.

Softest: the M4x and M8+

Both were set on days among the fastest 4%, by crews that were no faster than a typical Worlds gold medallist once the conditions are allowed for. A typical gold crew would beat them in about 1 A-final in 14 (M4x) and 1 in 17 (M8+), so both are likely to fall within about four years.

Close calls still happen

A New Zealand pair finished 0.54 s outside the M2- record at World Cup II in 2026. Crews have also come within 0.2% of the M2x and W2x records. Even a hard record can be nearly matched when a strong crew gets a fast day.

1 · Ranking

How hard each record is today

Typical 2026 gold is the time the model expects to win a Worlds A-final in the class, in average conditions, in 2026. Gap to beat is how much faster than that the record is. The chance per A-final combines that gap with conditions sampled from 505 real race days and the normal race-to-race variation of winning crews. It is calibrated so that it predicts the number of records actually broken in A-finals since 2005. Hard means less than 1 in 100 finals, and soft means more than 1 in 40.

2 · Anatomy

What made each record: the day or the crew?

Each bar shows how much faster the record was than a typical Worlds gold medallist of the same year, split into three parts:

  • Conditions: how fast that race day was. Every event racing that day is compared with its usual speed, so a tailwind shows up across the whole programme rather than in one result.
  • Stage: heats and semi-finals are raced at a slightly different pace from A-finals, so records set in them get a small adjustment.
  • Crew: what is left. This is how much faster the crew was than a typical Worlds gold medallist would have been in the same conditions.

6 · Unlucky crews

Fast enough, wrong day

Some crews never set a world best but were quick enough to on the right day. A crew's best-three rating is its average margin over a typical Worlds gold medallist in its three best A-final or semi-final results, with conditions and round taken out (the same rating as section 7). Only days no slower than −4% count, because the model fits the very slowest days worst. The day needed is how fast a race day the crew would have needed, rowing at that level, to break the record standing at the time. It is ranked among all 505 race days, so a 65th-percentile day means about one race day in three would have been fast enough.

Kenia Lechuga Alanis tops the list. Rowing at her best-three level, she would have broken Imogen Grant's LW1x record of 7:23.36 on about one race day in three. Lightweight crews take most of the top places, because their events are raced at fewer regattas and good crews get fewer goes at a fast day. Among open classes, Haigh and Scown (New Zealand W2-, 2011) needed only an 84th-percentile day to beat Romania's 2002 record. Two M2- crews were each one good tailwind from the pair record: Reed and Hodge against Pinsent and Cracknell's 6:14.27, and Chardin and Mortelette against Murray and Bond's 6:08.50. Of crews, would have broken the record standing in their time on a top-5% day.

7 · Generational crews

Who were the great crews?

Each crew's results since 2000 are measured two ways:

  • Best-three rating: how much faster than a typical Worlds gold medallist the crew was, on average, in its three best races, after taking out each day's conditions and the round. It rates the crew at its best across several races, not the top speed it reached at one moment in one race. Only A-finals and semi-finals on days no slower than −4% count. B-finals get a large round adjustment, and the slowest days are where the model fits fast crews worst, so both can inflate a single result.
  • Dominance: its average winning margin over second place in the A-finals it won. Both boats raced in the same conditions, so dominance needs no model at all.

Pairs, doubles and singles are tracked as the same people across seasons. Fours and eights change seats, so each is one nation's crew for one season.

Murray and Bond lead on both measures. They won all 24 of their A-finals in the data, by an average of 1.3%, and their best-three rating of +4.9% is the highest of any crew in any class. Chardin and Mortelette, the French pair racing in the same years, are second overall (+4.0%).

A record needs a strong crew and a fast day at the same time, so record holders are not always the best crews. Robbie Manson's M1x record shows how that works. His 6:30.74 at the 2017 World Cup in Poznań was 6.9% faster than a typical 2017 Worlds gold medallist. About 4.8 points of that came from the day, which was among the fastest 2% of race days, with a tailwind of about 3.7 m/s. The other 2.1 points were Manson himself, and that race is his best result in the data. His best-three rating, the average of that race and his next two best, is +1.4%. That ranks him 20th of the 34 single scullers with at least five A-finals since 2000. The list is nearly every leading sculler of the era, so 20th is still very good, but it means the record owes more to Poznań's tailwind than to Manson being the fastest sculler of his time. Mahé Drysdale leads the list at +3.5%. The eights tell a similar story: the M8+ record crew (Netherlands, 2026) ranks 39th of 100 M8+ crews, while the W8+ record crew (Romania, 2026) ranks 5th of 95.

8 · Next season

Which records could fall in 2027?

This takes the three best crews of 2026 in each class and their 2026 form, meaning their average margin over a typical Worlds gold in A-finals and semi-finals, conditions removed. Form is shrunk about back toward typical, because that's how much a crew's form carries over from one season to the next on average. The model then plays out about five A-finals with conditions drawn from real race days. The chance shown is that at least one of those crews breaks the record at least once.

10 · Countries

Who holds the records

Great Britain and Italy have set the most elite records since 1985, with 17 each, and New Zealand holds the most current elite world best times, with four. Per head of population, New Zealand is far ahead: its four elite world bests work out at 7.5 per 10 million people, almost three times Croatia, the next best. Switch the measure below.

Which lakes are fastest?

Each dot is one race day since 2000 at the venue, placed by its conditions effect, which is how much faster or slower every event ran that day than usual. The open ring is the venue's average. 12 · Wind

How much does a tailwind help?

I matched every race since 2000 to hourly ERA5 weather at the course, from the Open-Meteo archive. The data shows which wind direction makes each venue fast, which gives the course direction without needing a map. To keep that honest, each day's tailwind is measured along a direction learnt from the other half of the years. Each point below is one race day.

Across race days, wind along the course is most of what separates a fast day from a slow one, but the relationship is not a straight line. With separate slopes either side of still air, each 1 m/s of headwind cost about , while each 1 m/s of tailwind gave only about . A headwind hurts about three times as much as the same tailwind helps, which fits air drag rising with the square of the boat's speed through the air. A smooth curve (quadratic) shows the same flattening, and both fit clearly better than a straight line (). Crosswind made no clear difference. Each 1 °C of warmer air added about . Within a single day, races rowed in a stronger tailwind than earlier or later races were faster by per m/s. The 2014 Worlds in Amsterdam, with seven current records, had tailwinds of about 7 m/s during the finals. Murray and Bond's M2- heat in London had 2.0 m/s.

13 · Time of day

Morning or afternoon?

This compares winners' speed with other races on the same day at the same regatta, after allowing for class and round. Winners are fastest around 11:00 to 12:00. By 15:00 to 16:00 they are about 0.5% slower, as the average wind at these regattas rises from 2.2 m/s in the morning to 2.9 m/s. A stronger wind usually means rougher water, whatever its direction. Races at 9:00 are also slightly slow, in cooler air (about 18 °C against 23 °C mid-afternoon).

14 · From the records alone

What the world best times say without race data

Without race data, the only evidence is the records themselves. Boat speed should rise with crew size, so I fitted log speed ~ log rowers + women + lightweight + sculling + cox to the 26 records. Crew size gets an exponent of (theory gives 1/9 ≈ 0.111), with R² . Each bar shows how far a record sits above or below that line, with the class left out of its own fit.

This view cannot separate a strong crew from a fast day. The cox term is also fitted mostly on the old coxed fours and pairs, which flatters both eights. With race data, the M8+ comes out as one of the softest records (section 1).

Accuracy

How sure is this?

  • Typical gold times (section 1) are pinned down to about ± (95%), roughly ±0.5 s over 6:30.
  • Conditions on a race day (sections 2, 11 and 12) are estimated to about ±, because each day has around a hundred results. The crew part of each record inherits that.
  • A single A-final winning time, given the class and the day, is predicted to about ±, or about ±14 s over 6:30. That spread comes from the crews, not the model: some winners are far better than others.
  • Chance per A-final (section 1) is the shakiest number. Its calibration rests on record breaks, and a 95% range of to breaks The ranking table shows that range under each figure. The order of the classes hardly changes.
  • 2027 chances (section 8) were backtested on past class-seasons. The seasons the model rated most likely (top 10%) saw a record fall of the time, against for the bottom half. The model sorts likely from unlikely well, but the exact percentages are rough. Its Brier score is , against for guessing the average every time.

Method

How it was worked out

  • Data. from the World Rowing results API, elite classes only. The 2005 Worlds at Kaizu are excluded, as they are from World Rowing's records, because of a fast river current. So is the 1990 World Cup at Mannheim, where every A-final time beats today's records. Any race containing a time faster than the world best is dropped as mistimed, along with blank times. Mixed events are left out.
  • Conditions model. log(time) ~ class + race day + stage + place, fitted on the top three in every race from onward ( times, race days, R² ). The race-day effects also contain the long-run speed-up of about a year, and the depth of each regatta: . Both are removed, with a linear trend and a regatta-type effect, and what remains is the effect of conditions. "Typical gold" therefore means a typical Worlds gold medallist.
  • Chance per A-final. For a typical Worlds gold crew, this is the probability that its form on the day plus the day's conditions beats the record. Conditions are sampled from the 505 observed race days, and crew form is treated as normally distributed. In a backtest over A-finals since 2005, the raw spread of predicted record breaks against actual. I narrowed the spread to so that the backtest matches. Treat the chances as a ranking on a sensible scale rather than exact odds.
  • Crews and 2027. Every result also gets a crew effect: its margin over a typical Worlds gold once the day's conditions and the round are taken out. Crew ratings and season form are averages of those. 2027 chances come from each crew's 2026 form, shrunk toward typical by the season-to-season slope, with race-to-race spread calibrated so the backtest predicts the right number of record seasons.
  • Weather and splits. Hourly ERA5 reanalysis from the Open-Meteo archive at each course, matched to each race's local start hour. 500 m splits come from the World Rowing API. Results whose 500 m times repeat to the hundredth are treated as filled in and dropped.
  • Limits. A single linear trend covers all classes, so classes that have gained or lost depth (such as the lightweight events since they left the Olympics) are only roughly placed. Typical gold means an average winner, and a generational crew changes the odds while it is racing. Record days on which only one or two events raced, like the LM1x at the non-Olympic 2024 Worlds, cannot be split.
